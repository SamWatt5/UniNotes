# What is research?
- systematic process of collecting and analysing information to *answer* a question or *solve* a problem
- different from "finding things out" because its:
	- **Planned**: there is a design that links thequestion to the methods
	- **Justified**: choices about methods, participants and analysis are explained
	- **Transparent**: others can see what was done and judge the conclusions
	- **Grounded in existing knowledge**: it builds on, or challenges published work
	- **Ethical**: it protects participants and handles data accordingle
- In computing, research often aims to understand people and practices, build and evaluate artefacts, or test claims about systems
>[!question] Ask yourself
>- What is the problem?
>- Why does it matter, and to whom?
>- What do we already know?
>- What evidence would answer the quesiton?
>- How will I know my answer is trustworthy?
## Types of research in computing
1. Experimental
	- Controlled studies that test the effect of one variable on another, e.g. comparing task completion times for two interface designs
2. Empirical / field
	- Studies of real people, systems and settings, e.g. surveys of developers or interviews with IT staff about how a tool is adopted.
3. Design science
	- Building and evaluating an artefact (a system, model or method) that solves a real problem, and reflecting on what was learned.
4. HCI & user studies
	- Understanding how people interact with technology, using methods such as interviews, observation, think-aloud and usability testing.
## Quantitative and qualitative research
> [!info] Quantitative research
> - Aims to maximise **objectivity**, **replicability** and **generalisability**
> - Collects **numerical** data or measurements
> - Answers **what, when, where, how many** and **who** questions
> - Data: self-reported raatings, task times, error counts, logs, sensor data
> - Usually **deductive**: tests hypotheses using statistics

>[!info] Qualitative research
> - Focuses on **discovery, understanding** and participants' **perspectives**
> - Collects **non-numerical** data: words, images, behaviour
> - Answers **how** and **why** questions
> - Data: interview transcripts, field notes, diaries, video, documents
> - Usually **inductive**: builds understanding from the data

# Research paradigms
>[!info] Research Paradigms
>The beliefs about reality and knowledge that sit underneath every study

## Why paradigms matter
- A set of beliefs about the world and how we can learn about it
- **Ontology**: what is the nature of reality? is there one objective reality, or many realities constructed by people?
- **Epistemology**: what counts as valid knowledge> Measurements and facts, or people's meanings and interpretations?
- Your paradigm shapes your **research questions, methods** and how you **judge quality**
- You do not need to become a philosopher, but you should be able to **state and justify** your position in a projcet report or dissertation
>[!example] Example
>Two projects study a new student app.
>One measures whether it **reduces task time** (a positivist position).
>The other explores **how students experience** using it day to day (an interpretivist position).

>[!quote]     
>A paradigm that originated in the natural sciences. It rests on the assumption that social reality is singular and objective, and is not affected by the act of investigating it.
>- Collis and Husses (2009, p. 338) on positivism
>	- The research involves a deductive process with a view to prodiving explanatory theories to understand social phenomena.

>[!quote]
>A paradigm that emerged in response to the criticisms of positivism, It rests on the assumption that social reality is in our minds, and is subjective and multiple.
>- Collis and Husses (2009, p. 338) on interpretivism

## Comparing the main paradigms
| Paradigm       | View of reality and knowledge                                                                                                                       | Typical methods                                              | Example in computing                                                  |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Positivism     | One objective reality that can be measured; the researcher is independent (collis and Hussey, 2009)                                                 | Experiments, structured questionnaires, statistical analysis | Does auto-complete reduce search time?                                |
| Interpretivism | Reality is subjective and multiple; meaning is constructed by people                                                                                | Interviews, observation, ethnography, thematic analysis      | How do nurses experience a new records system?                        |
| Realism        | "A reality exists that is independent of human thoughts and beliefs" but it is shaped by social forces (Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill, 2003, p. 85) | Mixed methods, case studies                                  | Why does the same system succeed in one organisation but not another? |
| Pragmatism     | The resarch question matters most; use whatever methods best anser it (Creswell and Creswell, 2023)                                                 | Mixed methods                                                | Which features do users need, and how often are they used?            |
## Deductive and inductive approaches
>[!info] Deductive: theory -> data
>- Starts from existing **theory** and develops a **hypothesis**
>- Five activities: deduce a hypothesis, express it, **test** it, examine the outcome and, if necessary, **change the theory** (Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill, 2003)
>- Tends to use **quantitative** data
>- Structured and relatively inflexible, so alternative explanations are not easily explored

>[!info] Inductive: data -> theory
>- "A study in which theory is developed from the observation of empirical reality; thus general inferences are induced from particular instances" (Collis and Hussey, 2009, p. 335)
## The research onion
- Philosophy: positivism, realism, interpretivism, pragmatism
- Approach: deductive or inductive
- Strategy: experiment, survey, case study, ethnography, grounded theory, action research
- Choices: mono method, mixed methods, multi-method
- Time horizon: cross-sectional (a snapshot) or longitudinal (over time)
- Techniques: samplin interviews, questionares, observation, secondary data
### Peeling the onion: a worked axample
Project: How do first year computing students use generative AI tools when learing to program?

| Layer        | Choice                                                                           | Justification                                                                   |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Philosophy   | Interpretivism                                                                   | Interested in students' experiences and meanings, not a single measurable truth |
| Approach     | Inductive                                                                        | Little published work on this group, so themes should come from the data        |
| Strategy     | Case study                                                                       | One cohort in one module, studied in its real context                           |
| Choices      | Multi-method qualitative                                                         | Interviews plus short diary entries give depth and triangulation                |
| Time horizon | Longitudinal (one semester)                                                      | Use may change as students gain skill                                           |
| Techniques   | Purposive sampling (12 students) , semi-structured interviews, thematic analysis | Enough depth to explore variation in use                                        |
# Research design
## Research design: why and what?
**Why?**
- It makes sure you obtain the evidence to address the **research problem, aim and objectives**
- It is the overall **strategy or plan** for your research
- It describes and justifies the **collection, measurement and analysis** of data
- It can be **verified and reproduces** by others
**What should it include?**
- The **research problem** and **rationale**: what is the problem, why is it important and timely, and who benefits?
- The **boundaries**: what is the scope and what are the limitations?
- The **aim, objectives** and **research questions**
## Aim and objectives
>[!info] The aim
>- One overall statement of what the research will **achieve**, addressing the specific research problem
>- Concisely conveys the **purpose** and **scope**
>- Example: "To enable fourth-year computing students at the University of Dundee to make informed choices about their module options."

>[!info] Objectives
>- A list of the **milestones or actions** towards achieving the aim
>- Should be **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound
>- Start with an action verb: review, gather, analyse, evaluate, synthesise, develop, produce
>- Example: "To identify the factors students consider when choosing modules, through semi-structured interviews with 10 students by december"

## Writing research questions
Good research questions are focused, answerable with the time and access you have, and matched to your methods.

| Type        | Purpose                                             | Computing example                                             |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Descriptive | Describe what is happening (what, who, how many)    | What accesibility features do UK iniversity websites provide? |
| Exploratory | Explore a new or poorly understood area (how, what) | How do small charities decide which cloud services to adopt?  |
| Explanatory | Explain why something happens                       | Why do students abandon online programming tutorials?         |
| Evaluative  | Judge how well something works                      | How usable is the prototype booking app for older adults?     |
| Comparative | Compare groups or conditions                        | Do novice and expert developers debug in different ways?      |
## Three main types of research design
1. Experimental
	- Numerical data in a **controlled** environment
	- Tests whether an **independent variable** has an effect on a **dependent variable**
	- Participants are assigned to equivalent groups
2. Quasi-experimental
	- Compares **naturally occurring** groups (e.g. two departments)
	- Less control and more uncertainty than an experiment
	- Less manipulation, so results may generalise better
3. Non-experimental
	- **No attempt** to control or manupulate the setting
	- Studies naturally occurring events
	- Includes surveys (quantitative) and case studies and ethnography (qualitative)
## Experimental design stages
1. Sampling
	- Assign individuals to two comparable groups: by random sampling or subject pairing (matched pairs)
2. Pre-test
	- Measure the dependent variable in both groups (Solom designs leave some groups un-tested to check the effect of pre-testing)
3. Experiment
	- Introduce the independent variable to the experimental group but not the control group
4. Post-test
	- Measure the dependent variable in both groups and compare the results
## Example: testing an online training package
- **Scenario**: A remote Health Board training service wants to know whether a new online training package (independent variable) improves employee performance and service delivery (dependent variables). Performance is tested before and after the training
**Problems to consider**
- **Control**: how could you be sure the online training was the factor that made a difference?
- **Measurement**: how do you measure performance and service delivery so that you can identify an improvement?
- **Sampling**: what criteria would you use to match staff in the two groups?
- **Other factors**: what else might affect staff performance during the study?
# Case study as a research strategy
>[!quote]  A case study is an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, especiall when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident.
>- Yin (1994, p. 13)

## When should you use a case study?
| Question type              | Suggests                                                               | Example                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What, who, where, how many | Survey strategy: describe how common something is, or predict outcomes | How many helpdesk calls do we receive in different areas of the business?                                                                            |
| How and why                | Case study strategy: explore or explain                                | How do our clients pereive our new system?<br>Why do we get so many helpdesk calls on this topic?<br>How is ICT affecting small business operations? |
## Designing a case study
1. Research quesitons
	- Usually "how" and "why" questions
2. Propositions
	- A theoretical framework that says what to look at. Even exploratory studies need a stated purpose
3. Unit of analysis
	- What is the "case"? An individula, team, organisation, event, programme or decision
4. Number of cases
	- Single or multiple, chosen to reduce the risk of misrepresentation
## Single and multiple case studies
>[!info] Single case (Stake, 1994
>- **Intrinsic**: undertaken to understand this particular case
>- **Instrumental**: examined to give insight into an issue or refine a theory; the case is of secondary interest
>- Suits new or unique cases, testing a well-formulated theory, or a previously inaccessible phenomenon
>- Can be **embedded** (several units, e.g. departments) or **holistic**
>- Can be **longitudinal**


>[!info] Multiple (collective) cases
> - Tests whether there is compelling suppport for  a set of **propositions**
> - More robust but needs more time and resources
> - Uses **replication logic** (Yin, 1994):
> 	- **Literal replication**: predicts similar results
> 	- **Theoretical replication**: predicts contrasting results for predictable reasons
> - Aims for **analytic** generalisation to a theory, not statistical generalisation to a population

## Multiple sources of evidence
Using several sources triangulates findings and helps overcome the limitations of any one method
1. Documents & archives
	- Unobtrusive and exact, with broad coverage. Problems: retrieval, biased selection, access.
2. Questionnaires
	- Self-reported views and opinions; can help with sampling. Limited depth.
3. Interviews
	- Insight into perceptions and experience. Risk of interviewer and recall bias.
4. Direct observation
	- Shows what really happens, in context. Time-consuming, costly and selective.
5. Physical artefacts
	- Insight into culture and technical operations, issues of selectivity and availability.
6. Why combine?
	- "Any finding or conclusion in a case study is likely to be much more convincing and accurate if based on several different sources of information" (Yin, 2003)
## Example: collaborative information retrieval
Fidel et al. (22004) studied how three teams searched for information together: a design team at Microsoft, a design team at Boeing and a customer service team at Boeing
**Sources of evidence**
- Interviewed the **team manager**
- **Observed** interations between team members at meetings
- Interviewed **individual team members**
- Observed team members **at work**
- Reviewed **communication network diagrams** built from team members' reports
- Monitored **email threads**
- Examined **documents** related to the team and project
>[!question] Think about
>- What is the "case" here?
>- Is this a single or multiple case study?
>- Why were so many sources needed?
>- Could the findings be generalised?
>  To what?
## The case study protocol
- Guides the investigator in carrying out the case study. It contains the **research instruments**, **procedures** and **rules** for using them (Yin, 1994, pp. 64-65)
**Contents of the protocol**
- **Overview**: project background and a letter of introduction
- **Field procedures**: access, resources in the field, how to get help, thje data collection schedule, plans for unexpected events, and a pilot study
- **Case study questions**: the questions the researcher must keep in mindduring data collection
- **Guide for the report**: an outline of the final case study report, so relevant data are collected
>[!question] Why bother?
>A protocol improves **reliability**: another researcher following it should collect comparable data.
>It also forces you to think through **access** and **ethics** before you start.
## Case studies: strengths and weaknesses
>[!info] Advantages
>- In-depth insight into issues and opinions; reasons can be explored
>- Participants can ve contacted for follow-up questions
>- Large samples are not needed
>- Benefits from using more than one data collection method
>- May give unique insight into a previously un-researched area

>[!info] Disadvantages
>- Results are not statistically generalisable to a wider population
>- Small numbers limit breadth of viewpoints
>- Time-consuming to conduct and analyse
>- Responses can be ard to anonymise
>- The resaercher's presence may introduce bias
>- Value may be limited beyond the organisation studied
# Quality in research
## Judging quality: two vocabularies
Validity and reliability matter for **all** resaerch designs and methods, but qualitative researchers often use the language of **trustworthiness**.

| Quantitative term                    | Qualitative equivalent (Lincoln and Guba, 1985) | Question it asks                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Internal validity                    | Credibility                                     | Is the right thing being measured by the right method? Are the findings a believable account of participants' views? |
| External validity (generalisability) | Transferability                                 | Can the results be applied to other settings? Is there enoguh detail for readers to judge?                           |
| Reliability                          | Dependability                                   | Is the resaerch stable, consistent and repeatable? Is the process documented?                                        |
| Objectivity                          | Confirmability                                  | Are the findings shaped by the data rather than the researcher's bias?                                               |
## Checks that improve quality
1. Pilot studies
	- Test instruments and procedures on a small sample before the main study.
2. Sampling
	- Justify who takes part and why; describe the sample clearly.
3. Triangulation
	- Compare data from different sources, methods or researchers.
4. Member checking
	- Show participants transcripts or interpretations so thet can correct errors or add issues (Hirsjarvi and Jurme, 2000).
5. Audit trial
	- Keep a record of decisions, codes and changes so others can follow your process.
6. Reflexivity
	- Reflect on how your own background and assumptions may shape the research.
# Using literature in your research
## Reading research papers for their methods
- In this module we read papers to see **how research is designed**, not just what it found
**When you read a paper, look for:**
- The **research question** or aim
- The **paradigm** and **approach**, even if they are not stated (can you infer them?)
- The **strategy** and **data collection methods**
- The **sample**: who, how many and how they were recruited
- The **analysis** method
- How the authors address **quality** and **ethics**
- The **limitations** they acknowledge (and any they do not)
>[!question] Judging a source
>- Is it peer reviewed (e.g. ACM, IEEEE, Springer journals and conferences)?
>- Is the method suitable for the question?
>- Is it recent enough for your topic?
>- Can you reference it correctly?
