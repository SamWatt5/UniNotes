## Choosing a data collection method
- Data collection methods are *not tied* to any particular research design
- Most can be adapted to collect *quantitative* and *qualitative* *data*
- *Direct* collection: gathered fromindividuals through questionnaires, interviews or focus groups
- *Indirect* collection: gathered by observation or from document analysis
- Some designs use a *single* method (e.g. a survey); others use *several* (e.g. a case study)
- Choose a method based on its relevance to the topic, your *research objectives*, the *type of data* you need and your access to *resources* and *participants*
>[!info] Say vs Do
>Asking people tells you what they *say* they think and do.
>Watching people tells you what they *actually* do.
>The two often differ, which is why many studies combine them.

## Asking people: four methods at a glance
| Method            | Best for                                                  | Advantages                                                         | Disadvantages                                                             |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Questionnaire** | Collecting a lot of data from many people in a short time | Anonymous; cheap; consistentfor all respondents; easy to analyse   | Little depth; no chance to probe; low response rates; "first choice" bias |
| **Interview**     | Exploring experiences, emotions and issues in depth       | Can direct the conversation and ask follow-up questions; rich data | Time-consuming to arrange, conduct and transcribe; interviewer bias       |
| **Focus group**   | Exploring shared views and how people discuss a topic     | Group interaction produces ideas; several perspectives at once     | Dominant voices; harder to moderate; less invidual depth                  |
| **Diary study**   | Capturing behaviour and experience over time, in context  | Less reliance on memory; captures everyday use                     | Participant burden; drop-out; incomplete entries                          |
# Sampling
## Key sampling terms
1. **Population**
	- Everyone the research is about, e.g. all undergraduate computing students in Scotland.
2. **Sampling frame**
	- The list from which the sample is drawn, e.g. a class list or staff directory. Gaps in the frame cause bias.
3. **Sample**
	- The people who actually take part. Describe how they were chosen and who they are.
4. **Unit of analysis**
	- What you are studying: individuals, teams, organisations or events (see Week 1 case studies).
## Probability and non-probability sampling
>[!info] Probability sampling
>- Every member of the population has a **known chance** of being selected
>- **Simple random**: everyone has an equal chance
>- **Systematic**: every nth person from the frame
>- **Stratified**: random sampling within groups (e.g. year of study)
>- **Cluster/multistage**: randomly select groups, then people within them
>- Needed for **statistical generalisation**; common in surveys

>[!info] Non-probability sampling
>- Members are selected in a **non-random** way
>- **Purposive**: chosen becaus ethey have relevant experience
>- **Convenience**: whoever is easy to reach (e.g. classmates)
>- **Snowball**: participants recruit others
>- **Quota**: fill set numbers in each group
>- COmmon in **qualitative** research\
